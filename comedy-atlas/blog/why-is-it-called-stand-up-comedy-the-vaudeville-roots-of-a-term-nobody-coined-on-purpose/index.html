Why Is It Called "Stand-Up Comedy"? The Vaudeville Roots of a Term Nobody Coined on Purpose

2026-08-13 · COMEDY ATLAS

The earliest known citation of "stand-up comedian" in print isn't from Los Angeles or New York. It's a British newspaper column, from 1917 — three decades before the American trade-press usage everyone assumes started it. The format itself — one person, a microphone, nothing between them and the crowd but timing — is older still, and existed for the better part of a century before anyone in the industry settled on a name for it.

Before It Had a Name

For most of vaudeville's run, nobody performing solo comedy called themselves a "stand-up." They were "monologists," or simply a "single." Douglas Gilbert's *American Vaudeville: Its Life and Times* — published October 24, 1940, and reviewed that year by TIME — documents that era's own vocabulary for the acts later generations would retroactively call the first stand-up comedians.

Take Frank Fay, one of vaudeville's most influential solo performers. According to vaudeville historian Trav S.D. (Travis Stewart), writing on his Travalanche blog: "By 1918 he had established himself as a monologist, and by 1919 he played the Palace." Not a stand-up comedian — a monologist, the word vaudeville used for itself. Trav S.D. later expanded this history into *No Applause, Just Throw Money* (2005).

Further back, Slate's 2014 history of the format credits performer Charley Case with something recognizable as proto-stand-up in the 1880s and '90s — comic monologues without props or costume changes, a format some scholars call an early precursor. Whatever it was called then, it wasn't "stand-up."

The Paper Trail

The most rigorous attempt to date the term comes from Oliver Double, a comedy-studies scholar at the University of Kent, whose paper "The origin of the term 'stand-up comedy'" ran in *Comedy Studies*, Vol. 8, No. 1, 2017 — bibliographic record confirmed via the Kent Academic Repository.

As documented by comedy historian Oliver Double, the earliest traceable print use of "stand-up comic" is a June 23, 1948 *Variety* review describing comic Lou Holtz as working "at his trade of stand-up comic." The noun form, "stand-up comedian," took two more years to appear: as documented by comedy historian Oliver Double, an April 12, 1950 *Variety* item called a young Steve Allen a "so-called stand-up comedian who has been groomed for the big time on the Pacific Coast Network."

By 1952, the phrase had enough currency that etymonline records "stand-up comic" attested in reference to Danny Thomas. Dictionaries lagged badly — the OED's own earliest citation is reportedly 1966, roughly eighteen years behind the *Variety* usage Double documents, though that figure comes from secondary reporting, not a directly confirmed current OED entry.

A British Twist

Here's the complication that undercuts the tidy "American trade papers invented it" version. In a 2018 follow-up paper — "The origin of the term 'stand-up comedy' — update," also in *Comedy Studies* (bibliographic record via the Kent repository) — as documented by comedy historian Oliver Double, an even earlier usage turned up: a column in the Yorkshire Evening Post, November 1917, describing a performer named Finlay Dunn as a "stand-up comedian." That's a British usage, three decades before the 1948 American *Variety* citation usually cited as the term's origin.

If that finding holds, the premise of an American term slowly coined by American trade press is at minimum incomplete — British music hall may have been calling performers "stand-up comedians" a full generation before Hollywood picked up the phrase. Nobody has produced a single "coiner" on either side of the Atlantic; the record simply shows the words in use, gradually, in two countries' entertainment press, well before dictionaries caught up. Worth flagging plainly: these 1917, 1948, and 1950 quotations reach us through Double's scholarship as reported in secondary summaries, not archive pages independently pulled and read for this piece — a caveat that applies to the single most interesting fact in this story.

The Name Outlived the Room

Nobody invented "stand-up comedy." The industry just eventually caught up to what it had already been doing, in two countries, a generation apart, without ever asking a dictionary for permission.
