The Forty-Seat Marais Room That Manufactures French Comedy Careers

2026-09-07 · COMEDY ATLAS

If you want to understand how a comedy scene actually reproduces itself, do not look at the big rooms. Look for the small one everybody has to pass through.

In Paris, for fifty years, that room has been on the rue Sainte-Croix-de-la-Bretonnerie, in the Marais.

Three names, one room

The space was built inside a former joinery — *une ancienne menuiserie* — and it opened in 1975 under the name La Veuve Pichard, on the initiative of three young actors: Martin Lamotte, Anémone and Gérard Lanvin. The French Wikipedia record places it deliberately a few streets from the Café de la Gare and the Théâtre des Blancs-Manteaux, the acknowledged pioneers of the café-théâtre genre.

That siting is the first lesson. The founders did not look for an underserved neighbourhood. They opened next door to the competition, because in live comedy proximity is not cannibalisation — it is a circuit. An audience that can walk between three rooms in an evening is worth more to all three than an audience with one option.

In April 1978 a group of actors, directors and writers — Michel Bouttier, Sissia Buggy, Pierre Chevallier, Christian Pernot, Bernard Turpin and Christian Varini — took the room over and renamed it Le Point-Virgule. Varini eventually became sole director. After his death on 8 January 1993, Marie-Caroline Burnat, who had worked with him for years, took over.

In 2007 it was bought by Jean-Marc Dumontet, who runs several Paris venues including Bobino.

What it is for

The room specialises in *humour* and the *seul-en-scène* — the solo show. That specificity is the whole point. It is not a variety house or a mixed-bill club; it is a room sized and programmed to answer one question, which is whether a single performer can hold an audience alone for an hour.

The list of comedians who made their first steps on that small stage is the argument: Élie Kakou, Jean-Marie Bigard, Pierre Palmade, Nicole Ferroni, Florence Foresti. Those are not similar acts. What they have in common is the format, and the room that tested it.

This is a materially different career ladder from the Anglo-American one. A New York or London comic climbs by accumulating short spots on mixed bills until someone offers a longer one. The French path documented here runs through a venue whose default unit is already the full solo show. A comic is not building a fifteen-minute set that might one day become an hour; they are being asked for the hour, in a room small enough that failing at it is survivable.

The expansion, and what it costs

In October 2012 the operation crossed the river, opening the Grand Point-Virgule at 8 bis rue de l'Arrivée in Montparnasse, inside a former Gaumont cinema, the Bienvenüe Montparnasse — two houses of 430 and 220 seats.

That is the standard trajectory of a successful small room, and it carries a standard risk. The forty-odd-seat original works precisely because it is cheap to fail in. A 430-seat house is not; it needs names that already sell. A venue that scales upward has to keep deliberately operating the unprofitable small room that feeds it, or it stops being a career ladder and becomes a receiving house.

In July 2026, Dumontet announced that the theatre would take the name Scène Montparnasse from September.

The lesson for anyone programming a room

The Point-Virgule's durability comes from doing one thing. It did not chase sketch, or improv, or music, or stand-up showcases. It picked the solo show in 1978 and has been the place to prove one ever since — through three owners, a death, a sale and a cross-river expansion.

Fifty years of comedians have a first credit in a converted joinery because somebody decided what the room was for and then did not change their mind.
