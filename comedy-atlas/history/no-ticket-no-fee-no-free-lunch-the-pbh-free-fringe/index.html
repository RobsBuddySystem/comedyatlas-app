No Ticket, No Fee, No Free Lunch: Inside the Bucket System Running 745 Edinburgh Fringe Shows

PBH Free Fringe scrapped venue fees for a donation bucket in 1996. Thirty years and 745 shows later, it has rivalries, expulsions — and a hierarchy of its own.

edinburgh-fringe, free-fringe, pbh, bucket-shows, fringe-economics

Doing the Edinburgh Fringe as an unknown act has traditionally meant paying to lose money: hire a room, split the box office, and hope strangers wander in before the run ends. In 1996, Peter Buckley Hill's fix was blunt — no venue fee, no ticket price, just a bucket by the door and a speech asking for donations, launched at the Footlights and Firkin. Nearly thirty years later, Comedy Atlas's database counts 745 shows running under the PBH Free Fringe banner in 2026.

"Not Pay What You Want"

The problem wasn't abstract. As PBH put it to The Wee Review: "The Fringe participation fee structure is ridiculous and discriminatory. One week in a tiny free-admission venue costs the same participation fee as a full run in the largest, most opulent, ticketed theatre." His stated aim: "I foresaw a Fringe in which there was no pay-to-play except where it was advantageous for the artist." And he's insisted the model isn't discount comedy but a different transaction entirely: "Our shows are free. There's a bucket which the public can choose to use for donations. Our shows are not 'pay what you want', which implies a compulsion to give."

That distinction — free, not "pay what you want" — is the whole structure in miniature. The Free Fringe's rulebook still tells performers to "never be rude about non-donors." It's a small line, but load-bearing: the bucket only works if nobody at the door feels shaken down.

Gates of Its Own

A system built to route around gatekeeping eventually needs gates of its own. The Free Fringe's rules now include an exclusivity clause — performers can't simultaneously apply to rival free organizations (Laughing Horse, Heroes of Fringe, Just the Tonic, Frankenstein's) in the same year, though one-off guest spots are allowed. Offers are binding; ethos breaches carry expulsion. Full 22-day runs get slot priority over shorter ones, and newer acts get steered toward mixed bills rather than solo slots. None of it is malicious — it's the scaffolding an organization running 745 shows needs — but it is, functionally, a hierarchy inside a project whose founding pitch was no hierarchy.

The Fractures

The fractures back that up. PBH's early collaborator was Laughing Horse's Alex Petty, who — by Laughing Horse's own account — started producing free shows in 2004 after watching PBH's model, partnered with him, and split off to run a separate free programme by 2006. PBH's retrospective on it, told to The Wee Review, isn't institutional pride; it's regret: "I would not have collaborated in any way with Laughing Horse. That was my biggest mistake." A later flashpoint came in 2015, when the Free Fringe and a rival outfit, Freestival, clashed over the Cowgatehead venue — roughly 90 shows caught in the dispute, per Chortle — with PBH telling Freestival-booked acts on Facebook: "The slot you think you have is not real. I appreciate this will come as a shock to many of you." Petty, notably, offered some of the displaced acts extra space rather than piling on.

The Cost of Free

None of this makes free costless. Comedian Ian Fox, quoted in The Skinny, put the economics bluntly: performers typically turn a profit only in the run's final week, and even then, "we are measuring profit in terms of a couple of new car tyres, not a new car." The bucket didn't remove the risk. It just moved it — from the door to all three weeks of the run.
