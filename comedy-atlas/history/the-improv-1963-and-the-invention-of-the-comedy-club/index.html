The Room That Invented the Comedy Club Was Not Built for Comedy

The Improv opened in 1963 as a fifty-seat after-hours coffee house for Broadway singers. It took a year to book its first comedian and most of a decade to become a comedy club at all — and every club since has copied the accident.

Almost every stand-up room on earth runs on a format nobody designed. A small dark space, a brick-ish back wall, a microphone, a two-item minimum, and a running order of five to eight acts doing short sets for a fraction of the door. That template has a birthplace and a date, and the striking part is how little of it was intentional.

A coffee house for singers

The Improvisation opened on 20 April 1963 at 358 West 44th Street, at Ninth Avenue in Hell's Kitchen, in a room that had just been a Vietnamese restaurant. Wikipedia's account of the club puts it at fifty seats, founded by Budd Friedman with his future wife Silver Saundors. It was not a comedy club. It was an after-hours coffee house where Broadway performers could come down off a show and sing at an open mic.

Comedians were an afterthought. Dave Astor became the first comic to play the room in 1964, a full year after opening. For most of the next decade the club alternated singers and comics, and only "by the 1970s" — the timeline is vague in every account, which is itself telling — did it become a stand-up venue.

The accident that made it matter

What turned a small coffee house into an institution was not the room. It was who started sitting in it.

Talent scouts from *The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson* and other New York-based television programmes began coming to West 44th Street looking for bookable acts. Once that was true, the economics inverted. Comedians were no longer being paid to entertain a room; they were paying, in unpaid stage time, for the chance to be seen by someone who could change their career. The club stopped being a venue and became an audition.

That is the model the whole industry inherited. The list of people who worked the room while nobody knew them — Richard Pryor, Robert Klein, Lily Tomlin, Bette Midler, Jay Leno, Robin Williams, Larry David, Andy Kaufman, Joan Rivers — reads like a canon, but at the time it was simply the cheapest labour in New York. Karen Black, Debra Winger and Barry Manilow worked there as waiting staff and musicians. Established comics came in to "work out" material before Carson.

The franchise, and the split

The second Improv opened in 1974 at 8162 Melrose Avenue in Los Angeles, in a room that had been the Ash Grove folk club. Mark Lonow came in as a general partner in 1979 and the single room became a chain. Then, in 1981, the Friedmans divorced, and the settlement did something no business plan would have: it split the brand geographically. Budd Friedman took the Los Angeles Improvs; Silver Friedman took New York.

The original New York room closed in 1992. Friedman sold the chain to Levity Entertainment Group in 2014. The club that invented the format outlived its own founding venue by decades, as a licence.

Two corrections worth keeping

First, the Improv was not the first place on earth where people paid to watch comedy. Wikipedia's survey of comedy clubs traces Japanese *yose* theatres to a first venue organised in 1798 in Shitaya, Tokyo, and Moroccan comic performance traditions back to the 17th century. What 1963 produced was the specific American showcase club, and its two acknowledged pioneers are the Improv and Dangerfield's, which opened purpose-built in 1969.

Second, the room's most famous feature was never a decision. It was a coffee house with a brick wall and no budget, and the industry spent sixty years treating the constraint as a design language.

Every promoter who has ever asked a comic to work for exposure is operating a business model that was invented by accident, in a converted restaurant, by a man who wanted somewhere for Broadway singers to unwind.
