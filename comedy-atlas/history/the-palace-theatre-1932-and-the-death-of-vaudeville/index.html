Vaudeville Did Not Die of Radio. It Died of Five Shows a Day.

The most prestigious variety stage in America played its last week of straight vaudeville in July 1932 — after trying to save itself by doubling the number of performances and cutting the ticket price.

vaudeville, palace-theatre, new-york, variety, circuits

Vaudeville is usually said to have been killed by talking pictures and radio. The financial record of its most important theatre says something narrower and more useful to anyone running a comedy room: it was killed by trying to fix a demand problem with supply.

What "playing the Palace" actually bought you

The Palace Theatre opened at 1564 Broadway on 24 March 1913, funded by the vaudeville entrepreneur Martin Beck and designed by the Milwaukee architects Kirchhoff & Rose. Its opening was a flop. Tickets were $1.50 for matinees and $2.00 at night, which the public found expensive, and four days later *Variety* ran a piece mocking the whole venture under the headline "Palace $2 Vaudeville a Joke".

It recovered on the back of a Sarah Bernhardt engagement in May 1913, and by December 1914 the same *Variety* was calling it the greatest vaudeville theatre in America, if not the world. The theatre's history) records that from opening until roughly 1929 it was the flagship of the Keith–Albee organisation, and that to "play the Palace" meant a performer had reached the top of the profession.

That prestige was a currency, and the operators spent it. Edward Albee, who gained near-total control after B. F. Keith died in 1914, is recorded trading on performers' desire to play the room by forcing acts to accept smaller fees. The most prestigious stage in the business was also a mechanism for suppressing wages — a dynamic that has never left live comedy.

The machine

The economics were extraordinary while they lasted. A typical bill ran nine acts, twice a day, rotating every Monday. The Monday matinee was the brutal one: the harshest audience of the week, and an act that failed it was simply cut from the evening shows. At peak the Palace turned an annual profit of $500,000, with an average bill costing $12,000, and about three-quarters of revenue came from subscriptions.

Subscriptions are the detail that matters. The Palace's core income depended on people who came back constantly and therefore needed constant novelty. As early as 1914 *Variety* observed that the theatre was using up headliners at an alarming rate.

The squeeze

By the late 1920s the pressure came from three directions at once. The Great Depression cut discretionary spending. Film and radio offered the same variety at a fraction of the price. And the new picture palaces — the Paramount in 1926, the Roxy in 1927 — were direct competitors for the same Times Square crowd.

Then the ownership changed underneath the art form. Keith–Albee merged with the Orpheum circuit in 1925, the combine acquired film companies the following year, and in 1928 Keith–Albee–Orpheum merged with RCA and the Film Booking Office to become RKO Pictures. At that point the circuit's vaudeville houses became, structurally, movie houses owned by a film company. The booking office moved upstairs, then eventually to Radio City.

The last week

The response was to run the room harder. Popular bills were held over for weeks, which drove away the subscribers who had paid for variety. Acts demanded more money. In May 1932 the Palace went from two shows a day to four and cut admission prices; a fifth show was added afterwards. Attendance did not rise.

The last week of straight vaudeville at the Palace began on 9 July 1932. A mixed policy — vaudeville in front of a feature film — carried on for a few months, and the final vaudeville accompaniment played on 12 November 1932. After that it was a movie palace.

Even that failed at first, because the major studios already ran their own Times Square theatres. The historian Louis Botto is quoted describing the decades after 1930 as a constant struggle for survival, and the building spent the next twenty years flipping between film-only, vaudeville-before-film, and live formats. A 1949 revival attempt under RKO's Sol Schwartz produced Judy Garland's nineteen-week comeback in 1951 — the first two-a-day vaudeville there in nearly eighteen years — and still did not restore the format. The vaudeville policy was dropped for good in July 1957.

The lesson a promoter can use

The Palace did not lose its audience because variety stopped being funny. It lost the audience by responding to falling margins with more shows, cheaper tickets and less novelty — degrading the product exactly where its paying regulars valued it. Any room that answers a soft week by adding another show and dropping the door price is running the 1932 experiment again, with the results already published.
