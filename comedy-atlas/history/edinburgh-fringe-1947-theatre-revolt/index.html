Edinburgh Fringe: The 1947 Theatre Revolt That Built the World's Biggest Arts Festival

Eight theatre companies weren't invited to Edinburgh's first international festival, so they showed up anyway and performed wherever they could find a room — a gatecrash that, uniquely, was never shut down and never stopped growing.

The world's largest arts festival was not planned. It was a gatecrash that nobody ever managed to throw out.

An Elite Festival, Built for a Broken Continent

In 1947, Vienna-born opera impresario Rudolph Bing launched the Edinburgh International Festival to help revive European culture after the war. As HISTORY.com's account puts it, Bing's vision was deliberately high-minded: a curated lineup of classical music, opera, ballet, and serious drama, meant to restore artistic standards and knit a shattered continent back together through culture. It was, by design, closed — a programme somebody else decided you were allowed to see.

Eight Companies Nobody Asked For

That same year, eight theatre companies arrived in Edinburgh without an invitation and staged their own shows outside the Festival's official venues and schedule. Six were Scottish troupes, responding in part to a lineup that had no Scottish work in it; their productions ranged from *Macbeth* to T.S. Eliot to puppet shows performed in restaurants. Seven of the eight performed in Edinburgh itself; one company staged a medieval morality play, *Everyman*, in Dunfermline Abbey, roughly twenty miles away, because that was where it could get a room. There was no unifying plan and, per Wikipedia's account, the early years of this parallel festival are "largely unrecorded except through anecdote" — it wasn't organized enough yet to leave a paper trail.

Naming It

A year later, Scottish journalist and playwright Robert Kemp watched the same pattern repeat and gave it its name. Writing about the unofficial performances growing up around Bing's programme, Kemp observed: "Round the fringe of official Festival drama, there seems to be more private enterprise than before." The word had shown up once before, in a review describing *Everyman* as being on the "fringe of the Festival" — but Kemp's line, in 1948, is the one that stuck and gave the whole enterprise its permanent name.

Building Infrastructure Around Chaos

For its first several years, the Fringe had no shared organization at all — companies that called themselves "Festival Adjuncts" performing in whatever space they could find, competing for audiences with no shared box office or programme. The first fix was practical rather than artistic: in 1951, students from the University of Edinburgh set up a drop-in centre at the YMCA offering cheap food and a bed for touring companies, according to Wikipedia's history of the festival.

Formal organization came later in the decade. Sources differ slightly on the exact year — the Fringe Society's own page dates its founding to 1958 "in response to the success of this growing trend," while Wikipedia and HISTORY.com both date it to 1959, crediting Michael Imison, director of the Oxford Theatre Group, with leading the effort. What both accounts agree on is the shape of what got built: a Festival Fringe Society with a constitution that explicitly rejected any vetting or censorship of participating shows, a published guide so audiences could actually find the scattered performances, and the YMCA established as a central ticket office. Nineteen companies took part that year under the new structure.

That refusal to gatekeep is, by the Society's own account, still the whole point: "we're proud to include in our programme anyone with a story to tell and a venue willing to host them." No jury decides who gets to perform. Anyone who can find a venue willing to host them and pay to be listed can be part of the Fringe — the same rule, functionally, that let eight uninvited companies build the thing in the first place.

From Eight Companies to the World's Biggest Festival

The growth compounds almost unbelievably from that starting point. By 2024, per HISTORY.com's figures, the Fringe featured at least 3,746 shows from 60 countries, across more than 250 venues, drawing a ticketed audience of 2.6 million — making it, by a wide margin, the largest arts festival on Earth, bigger than the international festival it grew up alongside.

The mechanism that got it there is the part worth keeping: no single founder, no funding body deciding what counted as good enough, no cap on how many companies could show up in a given August. Just a standing invitation, accidentally created in 1947 by people who weren't supposed to be there, that the city of Edinburgh never got around to revoking.
