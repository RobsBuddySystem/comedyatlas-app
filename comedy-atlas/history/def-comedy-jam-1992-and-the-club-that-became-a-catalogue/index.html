Two Clubs, One Cable Slot, and a Decade of Stand-Up Careers

Def Comedy Jam ran on HBO from 1992 to 1997 and was built from two existing rooms — one in Harlem, one in Los Angeles. It is the clearest case in comedy of television not discovering a scene but distributing one.

def-comedy-jam, hbo, television, new-york, los-angeles

The standard story about a television comedy showcase is that it discovers talent. That is almost never what happens. The talent is already working, in rooms, in front of paying audiences, for years. What television supplies is distribution — and the difference between those two claims decides who gets credit and who gets paid.

*Def Comedy Jam* is the cleanest example on record, because its own origin story names the rooms.

Where it came from

The series was produced by Russell Simmons, Stan Lathan and Bob Sumner for HBO. The record is explicit that Simmons and his long-time collaborator Lathan were inspired by two existing venues: the Uptown Comedy Club in Harlem, New York, and the Comedy Act Theatre in Los Angeles, California.

They then brought in Sumner, a Def Jam Records executive and veteran comedy scout, to launch it.

Read the credits carefully and the structure is obvious. A record label executive, a television director, and a talent scout took a live circuit that already existed on two coasts and put it on cable. Nobody in that sentence invented the comedy. They built the pipe.

The run

The original series ran 1992 to 1997. Martin Lawrence and Joe Torry hosted the first six seasons, Steve Harvey the seventh, D. L. Hughley the eighth. It returned in HBO's autumn line-up in 2006.

The list of stand-ups it carried is a substantial fraction of the next twenty years of American comedy: Dave Chappelle, Chris Rock, Jamie Foxx, Bernie Mac, Chris Tucker, Mike Epps, Katt Williams, Eddie Griffin, Adele Givens, Sommore, Sheryl Underwood, Monique, Aries Spears, John Witherspoon, with Kid Capri on the decks.

On 10 September 2017 a Netflix special, *Def Comedy Jam 25*, was filmed at the Beverly Hilton, and shown on 26 September.

Why the format mattered more than the material

Three structural things were doing work here.

A house band and a DJ. Kid Capri gave the show a musical spine, which is a hip-hop concert convention rather than a comedy club one. It set the pace of the show and made the room's energy legible on camera — the hardest thing to transmit out of a live comedy space.

A single continuous brand rather than a rotating slot. Because the show was a name rather than a timeslot, its episodes became a catalogue. That is why a twenty-fifth anniversary special was commercially sensible in 2017: the property had accumulated value independently of any one performer, in the way a record label's back catalogue does. Simmons came from exactly that business.

Uncensored cable. HBO's subscription model meant the material did not have to survive an advertiser. Any account of the show's style has to start there. The performance register people remember was not primarily an aesthetic choice; it was what became possible once the network was not selling the audience to a sponsor.

The part that should be uncomfortable

The show made careers, and the careers were real. It also demonstrated how quickly a live scene can be converted into a media asset owned by other people. The Uptown Comedy Club and the Comedy Act Theatre supplied the format, the performers and the proof of demand. The catalogue, the anniversary special, the DVD box sets and the streaming rights did not accrue to those rooms.

That is not unique to this show — it is the default arrangement between live comedy and everything downstream of it. The reason *Def Comedy Jam* is worth studying is that, unusually, it left a clear enough record for anyone to see the transfer happen, and to name the two rooms it happened to.
