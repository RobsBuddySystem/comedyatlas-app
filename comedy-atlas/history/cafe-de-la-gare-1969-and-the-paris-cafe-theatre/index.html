A Paris Comedy Room Built by Its Own Cast, in an Old Fan Factory

The Café de la Gare opened in 1969 as a deliberately unhierarchical collective with equal profit shares and no admissions policy. Its founders included an unknown Coluche — and the category it invented, café-théâtre, started life as a tax classification.

cafe-theatre, paris, coluche, cafe-de-la-gare, collectives

The English-speaking comedy world tells its origin stories through club owners: Friedman, Shore, Rosengard. Paris tells a different one. The room that shaped modern French comedy was not opened by a promoter. It was built, physically, by the people who were going to perform in it, and it was organised specifically to prevent anyone owning it.

Built, not rented

The Café de la Gare was created immediately after the events of May 1968 by a group of friends who restored an old fan factory in the passage d'Odessa, near the Gare Montparnasse in the 14th arrondissement. The French Wikipedia entry describes it as a collective building site where everyone worked on the room itself, and lists the founders as actor-builders — *acteurs-constructeurs-fondateurs* — on Romain Bouteille's initiative: Coluche, Sotha, Patrick Dewaere, Miou-Miou and Henri Guybet.

It was financed by patronage rather than capital. Georges Moustaki, Raymond Devos, Jean Ferrat, Jacques Brel, Anne Sylvestre, Leny Escudero, Pierre Perret, Jean Yanne, Julien Clerc and Jean Rochefort all contributed, as did the satirical magazine *Hara-Kiri* and the Inno-Montparnasse store. The founders themselves put their own modest fees into the common pot.

The first show was given on 12 June 1969: a set of burlesque texts by Bouteille, Sotha, Gégé and Dewaere. The hall was not finished. The house slogan was that it was ugly, dirty and fashionable.

The theory

Bouteille, who described himself as a conscientious libertarian, explained the intention directly. He said they had grown tired of cabarets where the performer was alone and there was no company, and that in 1968 it became important to make a theatre that had not existed before — a theatre where forbidding was forbidden. He believed, he said, in a de-hierarchised group with an equal share of any eventual profit and no apprenticeship requirement.

Read that as an operating model rather than a slogan and it is genuinely radical: equal profit shares, no artistic director, no gatekeeper deciding who is ready. It is the exact inverse of the showcase club, which was being invented in New York at the same moment on the principle that stage time is a scarce reward the owner allocates.

Both models produced enormous talent. Only one of them let the performers keep the upside.

The tax joke at the centre of the genre

The most quietly revealing fact in the whole story is what *café-théâtre* meant. When it opened, the Café de la Gare presented itself as a café-théâtre — a vague term which, at the time, mainly satisfied a fiscal category. And, as the record dryly notes, no coffee was ever drunk there, and there were never any tables or chairs.

A genre that has now given France half a century of comedians was named after an accounting convenience. Anyone who has ever registered a comedy night as something it is not in order to fit a licensing or tax bracket is working in a tradition with a very respectable founding precedent.

What it produced

The human arrangements were as informal as the business ones — Coluche characterised the troupe's way of living as friendship in cohabitation, and the personal entanglements between Coluche, Miou-Miou, Sotha, Bouteille and Dewaere are recorded in the same breath as the artistic ones.

Out of that room came Coluche, who would become the most important French comedian of the century, and Patrick Dewaere and Miou-Miou, who became two of its most significant film actors. Today the Café de la Gare sits at 41 rue du Temple in the 4th arrondissement, with 450 seats — by capacity, the thirtieth-largest room in Paris.

It is now an established venue in the Marais, which is what happens to every successful insurgency. But the founding proposition is still the sharpest challenge in live comedy: the people who make the show could simply own it, and split what it earns.
