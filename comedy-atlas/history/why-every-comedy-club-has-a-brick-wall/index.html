Why Every Comedy Club Has a Brick Wall

The exposed-brick backdrop behind every open mic and Netflix special started as a cheap renovation at one or two clubs — and America's most legendary comedy club never had one at all.

comedy-clubs, the-improv, comedy-store, catch-a-rising-star, club-boom

Walk into almost any comedy club and a wall of exposed brick is doing the work behind the mic stand — on flyers, on Netflix specials, on backdrops that have never seen an actual brick in the building. But the club most people assume started that look — the Comedy Store, arguably the single most legendary room in American stand-up — was never a brick building at all.

A Renovation, Not a Design Choice

The brick-wall look traces most reliably to The Improv, opened April 20, 1963, by Budd Friedman and his then-wife, Silver Saundors, at 358 West 44th Street in Hell's Kitchen. The club's own institutional history describes the original space bluntly: "the tiniest hellhole in Hell's Kitchen, a bare bones conversion of a defunct Vietnamese restaurant that had an interior brick wall and not much else," per The Improv's own official history page. It didn't even open as a comedy club — it started as a food-and-drink room where Broadway performers could unwind, and Dave Astor is credited as its first stand-up comedian, in 1964.

Friedman — born June 6, 1932, in Norwich, Connecticut, and dead November 12, 2022, in Los Angeles, at 90 — didn't set out to invent comedy's visual identity. He inherited a brick wall because tearing it out and drywalling over it cost money a converted restaurant on a shoestring didn't have.

Three Clubs, Two Cities

New York wasn't finished producing comedy-club real estate. Rick Newman — born February 21, 1941, and dead February 20, 2023, one day before his 82nd birthday — opened Catch a Rising Star in December 1972, on First Avenue near East 78th Street, the second pillar of the New York scene alongside The Improv, per Richard Zoglin's *Comedy at the Edge*) (2009). No source confirms whether Catch a Rising Star's own room featured a brick backdrop — it isn't safe to assume it did just because it shared an era and a city with The Improv.

San Francisco had a head start on all of it. The hungry i was founded in 1949–50 by Eric "Big Daddy" Nord as an 83-seat basement room in the Sentinel Building, sold in 1951 to Enrico Banducci, who began booking comedians — Mort Sahl among the first, in 1953 — before relocating to 599 Jackson Street in 1954. Some accounts claim the hungry i's original room had exposed brick, which would predate The Improv by more than a decade — but that traces to a single uncited blog post, and it's unconfirmed: the hungry i's own Wikipedia entry makes no mention of brick at all. Treat it as an unresolved possibility, not a documented fact.

The Club That Broke the Pattern

Here's what actually complicates the "brick wall equals comedy club" shorthand: the Comedy Store, which opened in Los Angeles in April 1972 under founders Sammy Shore and Rudy De Luca before Mitzi Shore took over operations in 1973 and bought the building in 1976, was never a converted restaurant with exposed brick. Its building had a glossier previous life — Club Seville in 1935, then Ciro's nightclub from 1940 to 1957, a genuine old-Hollywood glamour room, then a rock venue called Ciro's Le Disc in the 1960s, where the Byrds were discovered. No source describes a brick interior there.

The visual language most people now associate with "this is a comedy club" didn't organically appear at every founding room. It started as an accident of cheap renovation at one or two New York and San Francisco venues, then got copied so widely — into imitators, spinoffs, every open-mic backdrop in the country — that it got imposed even on clubs, like the Comedy Store, that never had the original brick to begin with.

The Wall Outlasted the Reason for It

Nobody chose brick because it photographed well. It photographed well because it was already there, in a building nobody could afford to fix properly — and fifty years later, comedy is still borrowing the backdrop of a club that couldn't pay its contractor.
